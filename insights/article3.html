The Short-Stay Lending Market

Specialist lenders, 30-year DSCR terms, and a market measured in hundreds of billions of bookings. Short-stay lending is no longer a side product.

Prime Domain Capital Insights · November 2, 2026 · 7 min read

Every lending market starts as a workaround and ends as a product line. Short-stay lending has crossed that line. What began as investors persuading conventional lenders to look past the absence of a lease is now a set of published programs, with named thresholds, standardized documents, and dedicated teams — offered by lenders that do little else. The question for anyone building in this space is no longer whether short-stay loans exist. It is how the market is structured, who sets its terms, and where it is heading next.

Start with the demand the lenders are financing. Global short-term rental gross bookings reached $219.9 billion in 2025 and are projected to grow at a 5.3% compound annual rate through 2029, reaching $270.6 billion, according to the 2026 edition of the leading industry sizing report. North America alone accounted for $78 billion in 2025, up 4%, and is projected at $81.8 billion in 2026, up 5% — growth that outpaced U.S. hotels, which expanded a more modest 1% in 2025. The same report frames the moment as a new phase of scrutiny and structural growth: the post-pandemic boom is over, expansion is slower and more uneven, and tighter regulation, changing traveler expectations, and evolving platform strategies now shape the sector. Lenders, in other words, are financing a maturing market, not a gold rush — and their underwriting has matured with it.

The U.S. revenue picture, measured on a different definition, points the same way. One research house sizes the U.S. short-term vacation rental market at $43,857.5 million in revenue for 2025, rising to $100,107.8 million by 2033 at an 11% compound annual growth rate from 2026 to 2033, with homes the largest accommodation type at 45.51% of 2025 revenue and the United States accounting for 29.4% of the global market that year. That revenue series and the gross-bookings series must not be blended — they measure different things under different definitions — but together they describe a market large enough to support specialist finance and still growing fast enough to attract new entrants. The performance data underneath adds texture: U.S. occupancy of 56.9% in 2025, essentially flat on 2024, with supply growth at its weakest recorded rate in mid-2025 before rebuilding toward year end. A lender entering this market is not betting on endless expansion. The lender is betting on a large, stable, slowly growing income stream — exactly the kind of stream debt is built on.

On the supply side of credit, three lender shapes dominate. First, the dedicated DSCR lender: firms that lend only against rental income, qualify on the property rather than the borrower, and publish vacation-rental programs with 30-year fixed terms and no balloon payments. One such program reports more than $3.5 billion in DSCR loans since 2012, including over $990 million in vacation-rental properties, with purchase and refinance loans up to 80% loan-to-value, a 680 minimum credit score, and down payments of 20% to 25%. Second, the short-stay specialist: firms built specifically around vacation-rental finance, qualifying on actual short-stay history, projections, or market rents — whichever debt-covers best — with loan sizes from $100,000 to $3.5 million, purchase loan-to-value up to 85% for the strongest credit, typical down payments of 15% to 25%, and closings averaging 14 to 35 days. Third, the conventional lender, still present but narrowed: 20% to 25% down, personal income verification, and in many cases no credit at all for projected short-stay income. The market's center of gravity has moved decisively toward the first two shapes.

Program terms cluster tightly enough to read as a market standard. Minimum DSCR ratios run from 1.00 to 1.25, with 1.25 and above earning the best pricing. Credit minimums run from 620 to 680 depending on the program, with better terms at 680 and above. Down payments cluster at 20% to 25%, stretching higher for weaker files or seasonal markets. Reserves of around six months of PITIA are a common expectation. Loan sizes span from $75,000 minimums at one program to $3.5 million maximums at another, with portfolio lending available above single-property caps. And the income question — which figure counts — has converged on a standard answer: actual history where it exists, discounted projections where it does not, with the discount itself now a published term (20% for new investors at one specialist) rather than a quiet adjustment. Convergence like that is what a maturing credit market looks like.

Rates remain the least standardized part of the market, and the most misquoted. Dated lender-published guides from 2026 illustrate the range rather than settling it: one guide from March 2026 described rates from approximately 6.0% to 8.75% for residential investment properties, while another from October 2026 described program pricing starting at 5.8% on a 30-year fixed term, with short-stay files carrying their own adjustments. A third specialist publishes a floor rate of 5.75%, explicitly subject to daily change with market volatility. None of those figures is a current quote, and none should be repeated as one. What they establish is the structure: short-stay pricing sits above owner-occupied conventional pricing, varies with ratio, credit, down payment, and loan size, and moves — sometimes daily. Any marketplace or lender brand that publishes a single rate as "the" short-stay rate is selling simplicity the market does not offer.

Regulation is the force most likely to reshape the market next, and it cuts both ways. Tighter local rules — permits, caps, registration, and association bans — raise the bar for every file, since lenders now verify that a property may legally be rented short-term before funding. That verification protects the lender's collateral and the borrower's income alike, but it also means credit concentrates in markets where the rules are clear and stable, and thins where they are contested. At the same time, regulatory clarity is itself a product: the markets that limited new competition through regulation were among the occupancy winners in 2025, as constrained supply supported existing operators. For a lender, a regulated market is not a closed market. It is a market where the underwriting question expands from "what does this property earn" to "what is this property allowed to earn, and for how long."

Where does that leave a new entrant — a lender, a marketplace, or a brand? The market rewards three things. First, income expertise: the ability to underwrite nightly, seasonal income credibly, with published haircuts and local comparable data, rather than borrowing a long-term lender's forms. Second, program transparency: published thresholds for ratio, credit, down payment, and reserves, so borrowers can self-select before they pay for an appraisal. Third, a name that tells the borrower, before any marketing spend, that this is the place for short-stay loans. The financing exists, the programs are published, and the demand is measured in hundreds of billions of bookings. The remaining gap is not credit. It is clarity — and clarity, in a market this large, is a brand.
