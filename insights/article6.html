How Long a Short-Stay Loan Takes to Close — and What Slows It Down

Twenty-one to thirty-five days is the honest answer. The appraisal sets the pace, the permit check sets the trap, and the contract deadline is usually the thing that breaks.

Prime Domain Capital Insights · November 19, 2026 · 7 min read

The purchase agreement said 21 days to close, because the lender's website said DSCR loans close in 21 to 35 days and 21 sounded better than 35. On day 9 the appraisal still had not been scheduled — the only appraiser in the county who would take a short-term rental rent schedule was booked two weeks out. On day 16 the appraisal came back $18,000 below the purchase price, and the lender's underwriter asked for a second look at the income. On day 24 the title company found a short-term rental permit that had expired with the previous owner and never been renewed. The deal closed on day 41, with a price reduction, a rate-lock extension fee, and a seller who had already started showing the property to backup buyers. Nothing in that story was unusual. Every step in it is the normal shape of a short-term rental closing, and the 21-day promise was the only fiction in the file.

The honest timeline for a DSCR loan in 2026 is 21 to 35 days from application to funding. The fastest closings — experienced borrowers, clean files, cooperative sellers — land in about 14 days. Deals with problems drag past 45. The range is not marketing; it is the sum of four steps that run mostly in sequence, and the slowest step sets the pace for all of them. Day one to three is the application and initial review, including the rate lock — fast, because the lender is underwriting the property's income rather than the borrower's pay stubs, and there are no tax returns or W-2s to verify. Days three to seven is the appraisal order, placed through an approved appraisal management company. Days seven to twenty-one is the appraisal itself plus underwriting review and conditions. Days twenty-one to twenty-five is conditions cleared and clear-to-close issued. Days twenty-five to thirty is closing documents, final review, and the closing itself. That is the smooth version. Most files are not the smooth version.

The appraisal is the step that decides the timeline, and it is the most common source of delays and failed deals. A DSCR appraisal takes seven to fourteen business days from order to completed report in most markets — and three weeks in rural markets and high-demand periods, where appraiser availability is thin. The appraisal for a short-term rental carries an extra piece that a long-term rental appraisal does not: a rental survey, usually Form 1007 or its equivalent, that estimates market rent — and for a short-term rental, that survey has to annualize nightly income rather than take the peak month at face value. Two things go wrong. The first is value: the appraisal comes in below the purchase price, which is more likely on a short-term rental because the buyer paid for projected nightly income and the appraiser valued the property on comparable sales. The second is income: the appraiser's market rent comes in below the AirDNA projection, which drops the coverage ratio below the lender's floor and forces a larger down payment, a rate adjustment, or a renegotiation. Either failure costs a week at minimum, and the second one often costs the deal.

Underwriting runs parallel to the appraisal but finishes after it, and it has its own clock. Initial underwriting takes five to ten business days, and conditions — additional documents or clarifications the underwriter requests — add three to seven more. For a short-term rental, the conditions are specific: twelve months of booking history or an AirDNA report, proof that the address can legally host short stays, and the 1007 rent schedule marked for short-term use. The permit check is the trap most buyers never see coming. The lender confirms the property can legally host short stays, which means the permit has to be current, transferable, and in the right name — and in cities that require a license, an expired permit from the previous owner is not a paperwork problem, it is a loan problem. Title work runs five to ten business days in parallel, and insurance takes two to five days to quote and bind — longer in high-risk states, where short-term rental premiums have risen 20% to 40% and in some markets doubled or tripled. Clear-to-close typically comes three to five business days before the target date, which means the last week of the timeline is the least flexible part of it.

The comparison that matters is not against other DSCR loans but against the alternatives the buyer is actually choosing between. A conventional full-documentation investment loan takes 30 to 45 days, because it underwrites the borrower's income in full. A hard-money loan closes in five to ten business days, because it underwrites almost nothing except the property's value — and charges for the privilege. A short-term rental DSCR loan sits between them at 21 to 35 days, faster than conventional because there is no personal income to verify, slower than hard money because the appraisal and the income review are real. Some specialist short-term rental lenders advertise purchase and renovation loans averaging 21 days and closing in about seven once title and insurance are ready — and that is true, once title and insurance are ready, which is doing most of the work in that sentence. The honest way to read any lender's timeline is to ask what it assumes: a clean appraisal, a current permit, and a title search with no surprises. Those are assumptions, not guarantees.

The counterargument is that speed is a lender's marketing line, and the fastest close is often the one with the thinnest diligence. A 14-day close means the appraisal was fast, the title was clean, and the underwriter asked for nothing — which is a description of a simple deal, not a better lender. The appraisal is the one step you cannot pay to skip, and the buyer who pressures the appraiser for speed usually gets a report the underwriter sends back. The other counterargument is that the timeline is not really the lender's at all: it is the appraiser's, the title company's, and the city's. The lender orders the appraisal and waits like everyone else. Blaming the lender for a three-week appraisal in a rural county is like blaming the airline for the weather — it feels accurate and changes nothing.

The practical move is to build the contract around the appraisal, not around the lender's brochure. Thirty days to close is the number to write into the purchase agreement, with the appraisal contingency and the permit contingency written as separate lines rather than folded into a single financing contingency — because they fail for different reasons and need different cures. Order the appraisal the day the contract is signed, not the day the lender asks for it. Confirm the permit is current and transferable before the inspection period ends, not during underwriting. And keep a week of slack after the clear-to-close date, because the last three to five business days before closing are the least flexible in the whole process. A deal that closes in 41 days with a price reduction and a rate-lock extension still closes. A deal that was promised in 21 days and dies on day 30 does not.
