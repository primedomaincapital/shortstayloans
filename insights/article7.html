The City That Changed the Rules Mid-Loan: Regulatory Risk in Short-Stay Lending

New York cut its Airbnb listings by about 70% in a single enforcement. The loan on a legal listing survived. The loan on an illegal one did not — and the difference was decided in a council chamber, not a market.

Prime Domain Capital Insights · November 19, 2026 · 7 min read

On September 5, 2023, New York City began enforcing Local Law 18, and the short-term rental market in the largest city in America effectively ended overnight. Hosts renting a whole apartment for fewer than 30 days had to register with the Mayor's Office of Special Enforcement, be present during the stay, and host no more than two guests. Platforms could not process a booking for an unregistered listing. By August 28, the city had received 3,250 registration applications and granted 257. Active Airbnb listings fell by approximately 70% — from more than 22,000 to fewer than 3,000. Three years later, the October 2026 snapshot tells the rest of the story: about 24,000 listings ask for stays of 30 nights or more, and 96% of those ask for exactly 30 — the line where registration is not required at all. About 5,700 listings still allow shorter stays, and two-thirds of them are private rooms. The market did not recover. It reorganized itself around the rule.

The loan mechanics of a rule change are what make it a lending story rather than a zoning story. A DSCR loan is underwritten on the property's income, and for a short-term rental that income is nightly revenue. When a city bans or caps short stays, the income does not fall by a little — it falls to whatever the property earns as a long-term rental, which is typically 20% to 30% less, and in a market where the buyer paid a short-term premium for the property, the value falls with it. The coverage ratio the lender calculated at origination — 1.25 on nightly income — becomes something closer to 0.9 on long-term rent, which is below the floor on most DSCR programs. The borrower cannot refinance, because the new appraisal reflects the new income. The borrower cannot sell without a loss, because the buyer pool has shrunk to long-term investors paying long-term prices. The lender's remedies are the ones every loan agreement contains and nobody reads until they matter: the right to demand additional collateral, to call the loan, or to foreclose. The rule change does not have to make the property worthless. It only has to make the ratio fail.

New York is the extreme case, but the pattern repeats in every city that has moved, and the moves keep coming. Los Angeles restricts short-term rentals to a primary residence with a 120-night annual cap. San Francisco caps unhosted stays at 90 nights. Austin now requires a city license for every rental under 30 days, and since July 1, 2026, Airbnb and Vrbo have had to pull any listing the city flags as unlicensed — by April 1 the city had flagged 2,785 unlicensed addresses, and a license costs $836.30. Dallas's 2023 ban on short-term rentals in single-family zones remains blocked by an injunction, and the city has asked the Texas Supreme Court to lift it ahead of the World Cup. In Europe, EU-wide short-term rental rules took effect in May 2026, and Barcelona is eliminating short-term licenses in the city center entirely. None of these cities announced a ban and then waited. The enforcement mechanism is the platforms: the city tells Airbnb and Vrbo to delist, and the listing disappears the same week. A borrower who bought on nightly income can lose the income before the first payment is due.

The penalties are designed to make the platforms the enforcers, and they work. In New York, a host operating without registration faces a fine of the lesser of $5,000 or three times the rental revenue, and advertising an apartment for stays under 30 days carries civil penalties of up to $1,000 for a first violation, $5,000 for a second, and $7,500 for later ones. A platform that processes a transaction for an unregistered listing faces up to $1,500 per transaction, or three times the fee where the fee can be documented. The registration itself costs $145 and lasts a maximum of four years — but rent-regulated apartments cannot be registered at all, and a building owner can put an entire building on the Prohibited Buildings List by certifying that every lease prohibits short-term rentals. The borrower who checked the city code before buying knows all of this. The borrower who checked the AirDNA projection first does not, and the difference shows up in the same place: the lender's income verification, which now asks for the permit before it asks for the projection.

The insurance market is the second squeeze, and it hits before the regulation does. Short-term rental insurance premiums are up 20% to 40% in many markets, and in high-risk states like Florida, Louisiana, and parts of California, some investors have seen them double or triple. The lender requires proof of coverage at closing and can force-place a policy at the borrower's expense if coverage lapses — and a standard homeowner's policy does not cover short-term rental activity, which means the borrower who buys the cheaper policy has bought no coverage at all. The refinance is where the rule change bites hardest: a borrower who wants to refinance a short-term rental loan after a city has capped rentals faces an appraisal that reflects the new income, a coverage ratio calculated on long-term rent, and a lender that now treats the property as a different asset class than the one it financed. The loan that was easy to get is not easy to replace, and the borrower who discovers that at the end of a rate-lock extension has discovered it too late.

The counterargument is that regulation is also what makes the legal listings valuable. A city that caps short-term rentals creates scarcity, and scarcity is a price. The legal listings in New York did not lose value when the illegal ones disappeared — the nightly rates on the remaining legal supply rose, and hotel rates rose with them. The same logic applies in Austin, Los Angeles, and San Francisco: the permit is now the moat, and a property with a current, transferable permit in a capped market is worth more than the same property in an unregulated market, because the supply cannot grow. The risk is binary, not gradual — legal or not — and the binary nature of it is exactly what makes it underwritable. A lender can price a permit. It cannot price a maybe.

The other counterargument is that the rules do change back, sometimes. New York's Intro 879, introduced in April 2026, would let owner-occupants of one- and two-family homes host up to four guests, host without being present, and lock private rooms — and as of November 2026 it was pending in committee. In March 2026, four City Council committee chairs, with the mayor's agreement, declined Airbnb's request to suspend the rules temporarily for the World Cup. The lesson is not that the rules never change. It is that they change on the city's timetable, not the borrower's, and a loan that depends on a rule changing back is a speculation, not an investment. The borrower who underwrote the deal on long-term rent and got short-term income as a bonus is fine either way. The borrower who underwrote on nightly income and got a delisting notice is not.

The underwriting order for a short-stay loan in 2026 is therefore the permit first and the projection second — the same order this site's earlier article on the best year since 2021 set out, now applied from the lender's side of the table. Check the city code before the nightly rate. Confirm the permit is current, transferable, and in the right name before the inspection period ends. Ask the lender what the coverage ratio looks like on long-term rent, not just on the AirDNA number, and decide whether the deal survives the answer. A legal listing in a host city with a 1.25 coverage ratio at 7% is a good loan. An illegal listing with a beautiful projection is not a loan at all. It is a countdown to a delisting notice — and the countdown does not pause for the World Cup.
