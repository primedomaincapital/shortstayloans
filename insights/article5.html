What a Short-Term Rental Loan Costs in 2026: Rates, DSCR, and the Reserve Requirement

A strong file prices near 6%. A thin one prices near 10%. The difference is four numbers — and the lender runs them in the same order every time.

Prime Domain Capital Insights · November 19, 2026 · 7 min read

The cabin outside Gatlinburg was listed at $485,000, and the AirDNA projection said it would gross $4,100 a month. The buyer did the obvious math — a 25% down payment, a 7% rate, a monthly payment just under $2,500 — and concluded the deal worked with room to spare. Then the lender ran its own numbers. The projected income got a 20% haircut before the ratio was calculated. The reserve requirement came back at twelve months of payments, not six. The rate quote landed at 7.75%, not 7%. None of those adjustments was a surprise to the lender. All of them were a surprise to the buyer. That gap is the whole subject of short-term rental financing in 2026: the price of the loan is not the rate on the quote sheet. It is the rate, the down payment, the coverage ratio, and the reserves, priced together, and the lender decides them in that order.

Start with the rate, because it is the number everyone quotes first and understands least. As of mid-2026, DSCR loans — the product almost every short-term rental investor actually uses — generally price between 6% and 8%, roughly half a point to a point and a half above a comparable conventional investment-property loan. The range inside that band is wide and it is earned, not given. A strong file — 720-plus credit, a coverage ratio of 1.25 or better, 25% down — prices around 6.0% to 7.0%. An average file — 680 to 719 credit, a ratio between 1.0 and 1.24 — prices around 7.0% to 8.0%. A short-term rental or a two-to-four-unit property prices around 7.0% to 9.0%, and a file with a ratio under 1.0 or no ratio at all prices at 8.0% to 10% or more. The best tier anyone published in 2026 was 6.12% to 6.49% for a borrower with a 760-plus FICO score, 20% down, and a 1.25 ratio on a 30-year fixed loan. That borrower exists. Most buyers are not that borrower, and the half point between the best tier and the average tier is worth about $130 a month on a $400,000 loan — real money, but not the money that decides whether the deal works.

The second number is the down payment, and it is where short-term rentals quietly cost more than long-term rentals. The standard DSCR down payment in 2026 is 20% on a purchase, with 25% buying a slightly better rate. For a short-term rental, 25% is the realistic number, because the lender applies a haircut to the projected income before running the ratio, and the extra equity is what keeps the ratio above the floor. Weaker files pay more: 25% at 680 to 699 credit, 30% at 660 to 679, and closer to 30% when the ratio is under 1.0 or the property is a short-term rental. Cash-out refinances are tighter still, capped at 70% to 75% loan-to-value, which means 25% to 30% equity stays in the property. On a $500,000 purchase, the difference between 20% and 25% down is $25,000 of cash at the closing table — and that cash is gone from the buyer's reserves, which brings us to the third and fourth numbers.

The coverage ratio is the lender's real underwriting test. DSCR — debt service coverage ratio — is the property's monthly income divided by its monthly debt payment. A ratio of 1.0 means the rent exactly covers the payment. Most lenders set the floor at 1.0, some accept 0.75 with more money down, and a few run no-ratio programs at a steep price. The best pricing is reserved for ratios of 1.25 or higher. Here is where the short-term rental gets complicated: the income side of that ratio is not the AirDNA projection. Most lenders apply a 20% haircut to projected short-term rental income before calculating the ratio — some count AirDNA estimates at 70% to 80% — and many want six to twelve months of actual booking history before nightly income counts at all. A property with twelve months of operating history can often use actual income with no haircut. A new purchase or a newly converted rental gets the projection, the haircut, and the higher rate. The practical consequence is that two investors can look at the same cabin, the same $4,100 projection, and get two different answers: the one with a year of 1099-K history from Airbnb qualifies on $4,100; the one buying a conversion qualifies on $3,280.

The fourth number is the reserve requirement, and it is the one that kills more short-term rental deals than the rate ever will. Lenders typically require six months of PITIA — principal, interest, taxes, insurance, and association dues — in liquid reserves after closing. For short-term rentals, twelve months is commonly the minimum. On a property with a $2,500 monthly payment, that is $15,000 at six months and $30,000 at twelve — cash that has to sit in the account after the down payment and closing costs are already paid, and that most lenders want seasoned for at least 60 days before the application. Add it up on that $500,000 cabin: $125,000 down at 25%, roughly $12,000 in closing costs, and $30,000 in reserves is $167,000 of cash to close and hold. The buyer who budgeted for the down payment alone is $42,000 short before the first guest arrives. That is not a rounding error. It is the reason so many short-term rental purchases fall apart in underwriting after the rate was already agreed.

The honest counterargument is that the rate is the least important of the four numbers, and obsessing over it is how buyers lose money. A 6.12% quote that demands 30% down and twelve months of reserves costs more cash than a 7.5% quote at 20% down with six months of reserves — and the cash is the scarce resource, not the rate. The other counterargument runs the other way: DSCR loans are business-purpose loans with no consumer protections, prepayment penalties of one to three years are common, and the income the loan is underwritten on is a projection that the lender itself discounts by a fifth. A borrower who takes the cheapest rate on a property whose income depends on a single platform's algorithm and a single city's permit office has bought the wrong kind of cheap. Both objections are right, which is why the four numbers have to be run together, on the same property, before the offer goes in.

The underwriting order for 2026 is therefore simple, and it is the reverse of the order most buyers use. Run the reserves first — can you close and still hold twelve months of payments in cash? Then the ratio — does the property clear 1.25 after the 20% haircut on the AirDNA number, or does it only clear 1.0? Then the down payment — what does 25% look like against your cash position? The rate comes last, because by the time the first three numbers are settled, the rate is mostly decided: it is the price of the file you actually have, not the file you wish you had. A loan that clears all four numbers at 7.5% is a better loan than a loan that clears three numbers at 6.12%. The market in 2026 has more than 100 wholesale lenders offering short-term-rental DSCR programs, and the competition among them is real — but none of them will waive the haircut, the reserves, or the ratio. The cheapest loan in short-term rental lending is the one that closes.
