The Best Year Since 2021 — If the City Lets You

AirDNA calls 2026 the best year to invest in short-term rentals since 2021. Occupancy is 57.4%, rates are rising, and the World Cup is coming. The risk is not the market. It is the permit.

Prime Domain Capital Insights · November 9, 2026 · 7 min read

AirDNA’s 2026 Outlook Report calls it the best year to invest in short-term rentals since 2021. The case is straightforward: available listings are projected to grow 4.6% in 2026, still expanding but far less aggressively than the wave that flooded markets and compressed nightly rates from 2022 through 2024. Average daily rates are forecast to rise 1.5% in 2026, with further acceleration expected in 2027. The STR Premium — AirDNA’s measure of how short-term rental earnings stack up against investment costs — has climbed to its highest level since 2022. AirDNA chief executive Rohit Bezewada calls it one of the strongest environments for short-term rental investment in recent years. The midyear numbers back him up: U.S. occupancy is forecast to average 57.4%, above the pre-pandemic 57.0% baseline, with revenue per available rental up 2.9% year over year.

Then there is the one-time event. The 2026 FIFA World Cup is the biggest single demand event in North American short-term rental history: across 11 U.S. host cities and 78 matches, Airbnb projects $327 million in guest accommodation spending from an estimated 382,000 World Cup guests. A host with a legal listing in a host city in June and July is not competing on rates. The guests are already coming, and there are not enough hotel rooms.

The financing has caught up with the demand. Most short-term rental investors finance with DSCR loans, which qualify on the property’s income rather than the borrower’s pay stub. More than 100 wholesale lenders now offer short-term-rental-specific DSCR programs, including Griffin Funding, Angel Oak, Newrez, Acra Lending, Truss, Host Financial, Kiavi, and Visio Lending. Typical DSCR rates ran 6.0% to 8.0% in April 2026, and 6.50% to 7.25% for strong-credit borrowers by mid-year, a premium of 0.25 to 0.50 points above standard single-family DSCR pricing. The terms are consistent across the market: 20% to 25% down, a 620 FICO minimum with 660-plus standard and 740-plus for the best rates, a minimum coverage ratio of 1.0 to 1.25, with 1.25 or higher earning the best pricing. Lenders underwrite on twelve months of platform history, an AirDNA market projection — with a 20% to 25% haircut — or a long-term rent fallback. And the tax code helps: the One Big Beautiful Bill Act permanently restored 100% bonus depreciation for qualifying property acquired after January 19, 2025.

The risk is not the market. It is the permit. New York City’s Local Law 18 requires hosts to be present during every guest stay, and active Airbnb listings dropped approximately 70% — from over 22,000 to fewer than 3,000. Los Angeles restricts short-term rentals to a primary residence with a 120-night annual cap. San Francisco caps unhosted stays at 90 nights. Austin now requires a city license for every rental under 30 days, and since July 1, 2026, Airbnb and Vrbo have had to pull any listing the city flags as unlicensed; by April 1 the city had flagged 2,785 unlicensed addresses, and a license costs $836.30. Dallas’s 2023 ban on short-term rentals in single-family zones remains blocked by an injunction, and the city has asked the Texas Supreme Court to lift it ahead of the World Cup. In Europe, EU-wide short-term rental rules took effect in May 2026, and Barcelona is eliminating short-term licenses in the city center entirely. Insurance is the quieter squeeze: premiums are up 20% to 40% in many markets, and in high-risk states like Florida, Louisiana, and parts of California, some investors have seen them double or triple.

The honest version is that the data itself disagrees. Ask three data companies how Austin short-term rentals perform and you get three answers: AirDNA puts the average at $41,400 a year, 58% occupancy, and a $215 nightly rate, while Rabbu says $28,148 and 38% occupancy. AirDNA also shows Austin’s active listing count down almost 26% from a year ago and nightly rates down 25%. AirDNA forecasts U.S. occupancy easing 1% in 2026. Two lenders looking at the same Airbnb will often produce DSCR numbers 20% to 30% different, because of haircut methodology, seasonality treatment, and regulatory overlays. A projection is a model, and models disagree.

That is the underwriting order for 2026: the permit first, the projection second. A legal listing in a host city with a 1.25 coverage ratio at 7% is a good loan. An illegal listing with a beautiful AirDNA projection is not a loan at all — it is a countdown to a delisting notice. The best year since 2021 is real, and it belongs to the operators who checked the city code before they checked the nightly rate.
